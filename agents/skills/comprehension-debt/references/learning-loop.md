# The Learning Loop

*Load this when a reading agent needs to know WHY a protocol step works, or must decide whether a shortcut (skipping predict-before-reveal, skipping the mandatory line-read, accepting an unedited draft) is defensible on the evidence.*

## The crux: fluency is not retention

- Judgments of learning are normally made while the material is still visible — but the real test (debug this at 3am, extend this next quarter) requires retrieval when it is absent. In-the-moment ease is not a valid predictor of later unaided recall. This is the "illusion of competence." [Koriat & Bjork 2005](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Koriat_RBjork_2005.pdf)
- Classic demonstration of the inversion: repeated retrieval practice (study-test-test-test) produced 61% recall at one week vs. 40% for repeated restudying — but on an immediate 5-minute test, restudying looked *better* (83% vs. 71%). Whatever feels most fluent right after study is not what predicts durability. [Roediger & Karpicke 2006](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2006/2006_Roediger_Karpicke_PsychSci.pdf)
- Bjork's storage-strength/retrieval-strength split names the mechanism: retrieval strength is how accessible a memory is *right now*; storage strength is how entrenched and reconstructable it is later, absent the cue. Reading a diff raises retrieval strength for as long as the diff stays open on screen. It does not thereby raise storage strength. [Bjork & Bjork 2020](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2021/01/RABjorkELBjorkJARMAC2020ForPostingSingleSpaced.pdf)
- Dunlosky et al.'s review of study techniques rates practice testing and distributed practice "high utility" across learners, materials, and tasks, and rates rereading and highlighting "low utility." Reading a diff carefully, however attentively, is functionally a rereading strategy. [Dunlosky et al.](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/journals/pspi/learning-techniques.html)
- The illusion is not confined to lab study tasks. In a controlled trial, experienced developers were measured 19% *slower* with AI tools allowed, yet still reported afterward believing AI had made them about 20% *faster* — their self-assessment of their own performance was calibrated by the feeling of the work, not the outcome. This is the same metacognitive gap, observed on real coding tasks. The authors themselves later flagged this specific 19% figure as scoped only to experienced maintainers in large, familiar, mature codebases, and in February 2026 called the result "out of date" relative to newer models and practices — cite the mechanism (self-assessment miscalibration), not the number, going forward. [METR 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089), [METR 2026 update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)
- A Wharton study (Shaw & Nave, 1,372 participants, 9,593 trials) seeded an LLM with wrong answers and found users who consulted it followed the wrong path at a markedly higher rate and grew *more* confident, not less, after being led astray — the researchers term this "cognitive surrender," distinct from ordinary automation bias in that trust in the tool's fluency actively displaces the user's own checking. Exact percentages here are unsettled across sources — treat the direction (misplaced confidence rises, not falls) as the finding, not any specific number. [coverage](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QjetA7FyqI0)
- A preprint EEG study (Kosmyna et al., MIT Media Lab with Wellesley and MassArt) comparing LLM users, search-engine users, and unassisted writers found the LLM group had the weakest overall brain connectivity of the three; when LLM-reliant participants were switched to writing unassisted in a later session, they showed reduced alpha/beta connectivity specifically — networks tied to focus and maintaining thought patterns. Flag as an unreviewed preprint with a published small-N critique; directionally consistent with, not independent proof of, everything above. [Kosmyna et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872)
- Why this matters at the scale of a whole career, not just one diff: professional developers spend roughly 58% of their working time on program-comprehension activities generally, not writing new code, per a field study of 78 developers across 7 real projects totaling 3,148 working hours. Comprehension is not a side cost being optimized away by AI assistance — it is close to half the job to begin with, and it predates AI tooling entirely. [Xia et al. 2018](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/sis_research/3779/)
- Developers already sense the friction, though they rank it below speed concerns: in Stack Overflow's 2025 survey (31,476 respondents on this question), "hard to understand how or why the code works" was cited by 16.3% as a top frustration with AI tools — real, but well behind "solutions that are almost right, but not quite" (66%) and "debugging AI-generated code is more time-consuming" (45.2%). Comprehension friction is under-reported relative to its actual cost precisely because it doesn't show up until later, which is the whole point of the fluency/retention split above. [Stack Overflow 2025](https://survey.stackoverflow.co/2025/ai)
- Practical upshot: "I read the diff carefully" and "I understand this well enough to reproduce or debug it unaided" are different claims, and evidence says the first is a poor proxy for the second.

## Mechanisms that build durable models

### Reading code is a distinct, correlated-but-different skill from writing it
- Code-tracing performance correlates with code-writing performance, and "explain this in plain English" performance also correlates with writing — but correlated is not identical. A multi-institution study supports a skill hierarchy in which reading/tracing/explaining is a related but distinct precursor to writing, not a substitute for it. [Lopez et al.](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/10806/1/2008001530.pdf)
- Being able to follow an AI's code line by line does not automatically transfer into being able to produce equivalent code unaided later — the two skills need separate practice, and following is the weaker one to have practiced.
- fMRI work found that code-comprehension problems strongly recruit the brain's multiple-demand system — the same circuitry used for math, logic, and general problem-solving — rather than the language system, even though code reading superficially resembles reading prose. [Ivanova et al.](https://www.mit.edu/~hopekean/files/braincode.pdf)
- Implication: skimming a diff the way you'd skim a paragraph undertaxes exactly the active-problem-solving circuitry that would otherwise encode the change durably. Passive reading is not a lighter version of comprehension — it recruits different, weaker-encoding machinery.

### Generation effect
- Self-producing an answer beats reading it: mean effect **d = 0.40** across 86 studies and 445 effect sizes. [Bertsch et al. meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03193441)
- A larger meta-analysis (126 articles, 310 experiments, 1,653 estimates) confirms it: generation gives more than a 10% improvement in memory performance over reading, with the size of the effect moderated by "generation constraint" — how open-ended the task is — and stronger for item memory than context memory. [meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/s13423-020-01762-3.pdf)
- Boundary condition that matters for delegation decisions: a heavily constrained generation task (fill in one obvious token) yields a small benefit; genuinely open generation (write the function yourself) yields the full effect. Merely "typing something" is not the mechanism — deciding the content is.

### Testing effect / retrieval practice
- Roediger & Karpicke, above, is the canonical testing-effect result: retrieval beats restudy at a delay, and the gap goes the wrong way on an immediate test.
- Pretesting effect: guessing an answer before being shown it — even when very unlikely to guess correctly — produces better later retention of that item than being shown the question and answer together. [Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009](https://sites.williams.edu/nk2/files/2011/08/Kornell.Hays_.Bjork_.2009.pdf)
- Precisely quantified boundary: a 2023 preregistered meta-analysis found pretesting helps the *specific* prequestioned material by a moderate amount (g = 0.54, k = 97) but is close to useless for other, non-prequestioned material in the same lesson (g = 0.04, k = 91). The benefit does not spread — it must be re-applied at every decision point, not invoked once per session. [meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/s13423-023-02353-8.pdf)

### Desirable difficulties
- Spacing, interleaving, and retrieval practice slow or impair performance *during* acquisition but improve long-term retention and transfer relative to easier study conditions. [Bjork & Bjork, desirable difficulties](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-introducing-desirable-difficulties-into-practice-and-instruction-bjork-and-bjork.pdf)
- This operationalizes the storage/retrieval split as a design rule: the easiest path through unfamiliar code — read start to end, once — is the empirically worst path for durability. Difficulty introduced on purpose (predicting, retrieving, reconstructing) is the mechanism, not friction to be engineered away.
- Boundary condition: a difficulty is "desirable" only if the learner has enough existing knowledge to eventually succeed against it — the same underlying logic as the expertise reversal effect below, restated for retrieval-based rather than exposition-based instruction. A desirable difficulty imposed on someone with no relevant schema at all just becomes an undesirable one.

### Self-explanation and elaborative interrogation
- Students who spontaneously self-explained more while studying *worked examples* — i.e., someone else's finished, correct solution — built more example-independent knowledge, understood underlying principles better, monitored their own comprehension more accurately, and relied less on the specific example later. Studying a worked example is structurally the same task as reading AI-generated code; this is the direct precedent. [Chi, Bassok, Lewis, Reimann & Glaser 1989](https://education.asu.edu/lcl/publications/chi-m-t-h-bassok-m-lewis-m-reimann-p-glaser-r-1989-self-explanations-how-students)
- Quantified: a 2018 meta-analysis of 69 effect sizes from 64 reports found self-explanation prompts produce a weighted mean effect of **g = 0.55**, across problem-solving, worked-example study, and text-learning tasks — above the plain generation effect. [Bisra et al. 2018](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1186664)
- Elaborative interrogation — prompting yourself with "why is this true / why is this the valid way to do it" while studying — is the same family of why-focused active processing that drives the self-explanation effect above; treat it as the same lever, not a separate technique with separate evidence in this pool.

## Cognitive load theory and the worked-example effect

- Working memory is limited, and durable learning requires building a schema. Intrinsic load comes from the material's inherent complexity; extraneous load comes from how it's presented; instruction should minimize extraneous load so capacity is free for schema-building (germane load). [Sweller, Van Merriënboer & Paas](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1023/A%3A1022193728205.pdf)
- Sweller's original argument: unguided problem-solving, especially means-ends search, consumes capacity that could otherwise go to schema acquisition — the theoretical basis for the worked-example effect, where studying a solved example beats solving from scratch, especially for novices. [Sweller 1988](https://andymatuschak.org/files/papers/Sweller%20-%201988%20-%20Cognitive%20load%20during%20problem%20solving.pdf)
- This is the strongest evidence-based case *for* reading AI output: for someone with low prior knowledge of the exact area touched, a clean AI-produced solution is a worked example, and forcing unaided problem-solving instead can overload working memory before any schema has a chance to form.

## The expertise reversal effect

- Apprentices with little trade experience learned better from studying worked examples; as domain experience increased, the same worked examples became redundant, and problem-solving — doing it yourself — became the *superior* learning condition. [Kalyuga, Chandler, Tuovinen & Sweller 2001, J. Educ. Psychol. 93(3):579-588](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ640537&pg=117987&q=the)
- Kalyuga's broader review generalizes this to an interaction between prior knowledge and instructional technique: support that helps low-knowledge learners can lose effectiveness, or actively harm, more proficient learners, because the guidance itself becomes redundant load rather than useful scaffolding. [Kalyuga 2007](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/EDC608/Kalyuga2007_Article_ExpertiseReversalEffectAndItsI.pdf)
- Direct implication, spelled out: whether reading AI-written code helps or harms is not settled by a universal rule — it is settled by the reader's *prior expertise in that specific area*. A novice facing an unfamiliar library is in the CLT-favorable condition above (worked example helps). An expert working in code they already deeply understand is in the opposite condition — letting AI do it *forfeits* their own superior learning condition, doing it themselves, for no comprehension benefit. This is the mechanistic justification for switching mode per task by expertise-in-that-area rather than applying one policy everywhere.

## Prior automation-and-skill natural experiments

- GPS and spatial memory: across 50 regular drivers, greater lifetime GPS use predicted worse performance navigating without it; in a 3-year follow-up of 13 participants, greater GPS use associated with steeper decline in hippocampal-dependent spatial memory. Small follow-up N — associational, not a causal atrophy proof. [Dahmani & Bohbot 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-62877-0)
- A mechanism, not just a correlation: in 24 volunteers navigating virtual London, hippocampus and prefrontal cortex activity rose during self-guided navigation but showed no comparable rise when participants simply followed satnav directions. The relevant systems don't activate when the tool does the wayfinding. [Javadi et al., UCL 2017](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2017/03/170321122526.htm)
- Google effect / cognitive offloading: when people expect information to be saved and retrievable, they recall less of the *content* but remember better *where to find it* — a transactive-memory pattern. [Sparrow, Liu & Wegner 2011, Science](https://www.science.org/doi/epdf/10.1126/science.1207745) Directly analogous to knowing which file the logic lives in without knowing what the logic does — a weaker basis for debugging it later.
- Extended-mind counterargument, given real weight: offloading to a reliable tool is not automatically a defect — this is normal, and mostly fine. But Clark & Chalmers's own criteria for a tool to count as genuinely extended cognition require it to be constantly available, easily accessed, and *automatically trusted* by prior track record (their Otto/notebook case). Critics note that mere causal coupling to a tool doesn't make it part of your cognition — the "coupling-constitution fallacy." [critique](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.02061/full) Ad hoc AI code generation, prompted fresh each time with no track record on this exact codebase, frequently fails the reliability/integration/endorsement conditions the extended-mind defense actually requires — so the GPS-style "offloading is fine" analogy does not automatically transfer to it.
- Calculators and spellcheck, checked directly, do not tell as clean an atrophy story as GPS and satnav do: meta-analyses found no general computational-skill decline from calculator use except in one specific grade band ([Hembree & Dessart 1986](https://pubs.nctm.org/abstract/journals/jrme/17/2/article-p83.xml); [Ellington 2003](https://www.jstor.org/stable/30034795)), and a 2024 FSE study found IDE-autocomplete users scored *higher*, not lower, on API-knowledge tests ([FSE 2024](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~mcoblenz/assets/pdf/fse24-autocomplete.pdf)). This is real counter-evidence, not a footnote: offloading a mechanical sub-skill to a well-scoped, reliable tool has repeatedly failed to produce the skill decay people expect, consistent with what expertise-reversal predicts for narrow, low-intrinsic-load tasks. It does not extend to the higher-intrinsic-load, open-ended tasks (navigating unfamiliar space, writing/understanding non-trivial code) where GPS and the Anthropic RCT do show measured decline.

## Automation bias: why "just review carefully" doesn't fix this

- A foundational human-factors review found automation complacency occurs under multiple-task load, and automation bias appears in both novices *and* experts — training and instructions alone do not reliably eliminate it. Imperfect decision aids produce both omission errors (missing a problem because the aid didn't flag it) and commission errors (accepting a wrong suggestion because the aid offered it). [Parasuraman & Manzey 2010](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Raja-Parasuraman/publication/47792928_Complacency_and_Bias_in_Human_Use_of_Automation_An_Attentional_Integration/links/09e4150c09890db4c6000000/Complacency-and-Bias-in-Human-Use-of-Automation-An-Attentional-Integration.pdf)
- This is the mechanistic reason a protocol cannot rely on "review the AI's output attentively" as its safety net: the bias is not a discipline failure fixable by trying harder, it is a documented attentional effect that persists in expert reviewers under exactly the conditions AI-assisted work creates (high output volume, imperfect but usually-right suggestions). It is the argument for structural gates — a mandatory line-read on named risk categories, a hard requirement that a plan carry the human's own edits — rather than a reminder to concentrate.
- Anthropic's own coding-agent guidance converges on the same conclusion from the practitioner side, independent of the psychology literature: without a runnable check, "you become the verification loop: every mistake waits for you to notice it." A pass/fail check (test, build, screenshot diff) catches what attentive reading reliably misses, because it doesn't depend on sustained human vigilance at all. [Claude Code best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)
- Practitioner writing independently names the same failure mode: coding assistants can amplify bad or outdated patterns already in a codebase and create "poisoned context," producing review fatigue and complacency in the human reviewer specifically — the human-factors finding above, observed directly in software review rather than inferred from aviation/cockpit studies. [Böckeler, Thoughtworks](https://martinfowler.com/articles/exploring-gen-ai/04-coding-assistance-how-in-the-way.html)

## Deliberate practice: a real but overstated lever

- Deliberate practice's explanatory power varies enormously by domain: a meta-analysis found it explained 26% of skill variance in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, only 4% in education, and less than 1% in professions. [Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald 2014](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/2014-macnamara.pdf)
- A direct replication of Ericsson's flagship violinist study — the empirical anchor for deliberate-practice theory — found a much smaller effect than originally reported. [Macnamara & Maitra 2019](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rsos/article/6/8/190327/68523/The-role-of-deliberate-practice-in-expert)
- Caution this implies for the protocol: "spend more raw hours coding by hand" is not, by itself, a strongly evidenced lever in a domain (professional software engineering) where deliberate practice's explanatory power is at its weakest in this literature. The specific, targeted actions in the ranked table below — not accumulated hands-on-keyboard time as such — are what the evidence actually supports.

## 2024-2026 studies: AI assistance and measured learning outcomes

- PNAS 2025 field experiment, ~1,000 high-school math students: GPT-4 access raised practice-session grades 48% (unrestricted "GPT Base") to 127% (guardrailed "GPT Tutor") — but with AI access removed for a later unaided test, students who had used GPT Base scored **17% lower** than students who never had AI access at all. Improved immediate performance and degraded later unaided performance, in the same study. [PNAS 2025](https://www.pnas.org/doi/pdf/10.1073/pnas.2422633122)
- Anthropic's 2026 RCT, 52 mostly-junior engineers working in an unfamiliar Python library: AI-assisted participants scored 50% vs. 67% for hand-coders on a post-task quiz of debugging, code-reading, and conceptual understanding — described as "nearly two letter grades" — with no statistically significant efficiency gain from using AI. Code-writing questions were deliberately excluded to avoid syntax confounds. [Anthropic 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills)
- Mechanism detail from the same study: of six identified AI-interaction patterns, the three built on cognitive engagement — asking follow-up questions, requesting explanations, posing conceptual questions while still coding independently — preserved learning outcomes despite AI use; patterns of pure delegation didn't. Clusters that relied heavily on AI code generation averaged under 40% on the quiz. Authors flag the small sample and note it's unresolved whether quiz score predicts longer-term skill. [arXiv 2601.20245](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20245)
- A 2026 meta-analysis of GenAI in programming education (23 studies) found a positive effect on productivity (g = 0.33) but no significant effect on learning outcomes (g = 0.14, 95% CI [-0.18, 0.47]) — productivity and learning are decoupled at meta-analytic scale, not just in one study. [arXiv 2605.04779](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.04779)
- A 2026 qualitative study of 621 reflective diaries from 207 students over eight weeks names the accumulation mechanism directly: AI-as-black-box acceptance, context-mismatch debt, dependency-induced atrophy, verification-bypass. [arXiv 2604.13277](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.13277)
- Transfer caveat, stated plainly: everything above except the Anthropic RCT and METR's finding is classroom/education research on students, not professional software engineering. Applying it to professional AI-assisted coding is an inference from a structurally similar task, not a direct finding. The Anthropic RCT is the one study here that is actually developers, actually coding, actually with AI assistance.
- Comprehension debt and code-quality debt are different constructs, and evidence for one is not evidence for the other: a separate controlled study with 151 professional developers found no significant difference in *downstream maintainability* (code health, test coverage, SPACE-framework metrics) between AI-assisted and non-AI-assisted code when a second, independent team later maintained it — a null result on the artifact. The Anthropic RCT, run on people rather than artifacts, found a real, measured 17-point comprehension gap in the *human* who used AI to build it. A codebase can pass every quality gate while the engineer who "wrote" it with AI assistance still cannot explain it — these are two separate risks, and closing one does not close the other. [Farley et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.00788)
- Honesty about the construct itself: "comprehension" is not rigorously, uniformly operationalized across this literature — most program-comprehension studies define the construct implicitly by whatever task the experiment happens to measure (a quiz, a trace, a bug-fix). [Wyrich 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.11301) A review of validity-threat discussions across 83 human-centric software-engineering experiments found that of 409 mentions of validity threats, only 31 cited supporting evidence, and for the three most-invoked threats, 17 of 18 cited studies didn't actually meet evidence criteria on inspection; separately, three-fifths of text segments labeled "construct validity" discussion in that corpus weren't actually about construct validity. [Muñoz Barón et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.10563), [Sjøberg & Bergersen](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.05336) Treat every specific number in this file as evidence about *a* measurable proxy for comprehension in *that* study's task, not as a settled, portable definition of comprehension itself.

## Ranked actions: converting AI output into a durable model

Ranked by evidence strength against time cost. This ranking is itself a synthesis across analogous domains (worked examples, tutoring, Parsons problems) plus the two AI-specific studies above — no single experiment pit all six against each other directly.

| Rank | Action | Evidence | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Predict-before-reveal** — guess the approach/output before reading | Strong, precisely quantified (g=0.54 for the guessed item, [meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/s13423-023-02353-8.pdf)) | Low — one guess per decision point |
| 2 | **Self-explain-while-reading** — ask "why is this valid, what would break it" | Strong (g=0.55, [Bisra et al.](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1186664); validated on worked examples directly, [Chi et al. 1989](https://education.asu.edu/lcl/publications/chi-m-t-h-bassok-m-lewis-m-reimann-p-glaser-r-1989-self-explanations-how-students)) | Low-moderate |
| 3 | **Retrieval quiz** — close the code, reconstruct it from memory, then check | Strong classic effect (21-point gap at one week, [Roediger & Karpicke](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2006/2006_Roediger_Karpicke_PsychSci.pdf)), not coding-specific | Moderate |
| 4 | **Modify-it** — actively rearrange/reconstruct rather than read end to end | Moderate, coding-specific: Parsons-problem reordering matched full-rewrite retention in less time ([Parsons problems study](https://www.cs.uni.edu/~schafer/cohort24/Methods/materials/w4/pieces/ParsonsProblems.pdf)); a 237-student classroom study found *faded* Parsons problems (partial reorder plus fill-in-the-blank) outperformed both plain code writing and plain code tracing for learning patterns, and were students' preferred format of the three ([Parsons/faded study](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3411764.3445228)); AI-generated reconstruction puzzles beat reading a full AI solution on recall ([CodeTailor](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.12125v2)) | Moderate |
| 5 | **Teach-it-back** — explain the code to someone/something else | Moderate, conditional: benefit is near zero (g=-0.02) unless you decided to teach *before* studying, and only g=0.48 even then ([Kobayashi 2024 meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10648-024-09871-4.pdf)); the popular "teach and retain 90%" figure has no traceable empirical source and should not be used ([learning-pyramid critique](https://paperzz.com/doc/7254222/learning-by-teaching.-evidence-and-implications-as-a-peda...)) | Moderate-high |
| 6 | **Rewrite-from-scratch** — implement your own version unaided | Highest ceiling per generation-effect and expertise-reversal logic ([Kalyuga et al. 2001](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ640537&pg=117987&q=the)), but not reading-specific evidence, and can backfire for genuine novices facing high intrinsic load without prior scaffolding | Highest |

- **Best ratio: predict-before-reveal.** It is the cheapest of the six (one guess, made before opening the diff), it has the most precisely quantified boundary condition of anything in this pool (g=0.54 for the guessed item vs. g=0.04 for everything else), and it maps onto the exact moment a reader opens AI output — before fluency has a chance to contaminate the judgment. Its cost is discipline, not time: it must be repeated at every material decision point in the code, since the benefit is item-specific and does not spread to parts you didn't predict.
- Second-best per-minute: self-explanation, because it is the one lever the Anthropic RCT independently confirmed on real AI-assisted coding — participants who asked why, not just what, kept their comprehension scores up despite using AI.
- Everything below rank 2 trades more time for a real but harder-to-target benefit; rewrite-from-scratch has the strongest theoretical ceiling but the worst evidence-to-cost ratio for a reading task specifically, since it stops being a reading task at all.

## Why the ranking doesn't apply uniformly to all code

- All six actions cost real time, and the desirable-difficulties logic above only pays off if the resulting model will actually be used again. Median code-line lifespan across 89 repositories and 3.3 billion line/token lifetime events is roughly 2.4 years — but lines that do get deleted have a median lifespan of only 95.7 days in one 2026 study of 120 TypeScript repos. [Spinellis, Louridas & Kechagia 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7959608/), [2026 study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.04993)
- This is the evidentiary basis for scoping effort by how long code will last, not applying every gate to every diff: building a durable model of code with a 95-day expected lifespan is spending desirable-difficulty effort on a memory that will never be retrieved, because the code it would let you debug won't exist by the time you'd need it. The mechanisms above are real and worth their cost only when LASTS is true — the reversal effect, the retrieval-strength/storage-strength split, and the deliberate-practice caution all point the same way: effort should track expected reuse, not be spent as a uniform tax on every AI-assisted change.
